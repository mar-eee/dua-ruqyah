## Text and source decisions

- Preserved the supplied `<ar1>توافق الداء</ar1>` block in record 183
  verbatim, all structural tags, IDs, order, references and technical drug
  names. The 37 source records span 41 parts and have 69 non-null text fields in these chunks,
  including headings, and all are filled. One wrong source reference in
  record 169 was corrected and documented below.
- Record 165 broadens the Quranic idea in
  [43:36](https://quran.com/43/36) into a blanket statement about all
  non-Muslims. Urdu presents this as the author's interpretation, not a
  universal diagnosis. The claimed prohibition on a non-Muslim touching an
  Arabic mushaf is identified as a legal opinion, not an uncontested rule.
- Record 166's Bukhari 5652 report about the woman with epilepsy was
  checked against [Bukhari 5652](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5652).
  Urdu does not imply that patience requires abandoning epilepsy treatment.
  The source's cancer comparison is translated as an analogy, not guidance
  on cancer treatment.
- Record 169 cites `Bukhari 5371` for cupping, but
  [that report](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5371) is about a deceased
  debtor. The Urdu citation is `Bukhari 5696`, whose
  [cupping report](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5696) is relevant.
  `GLOSSARY.json` records the correction in a field-specific number
  override. Some further unnumbered cupping quotations in record 169
  need a dedicated hadith-attribution review before publication.
- Records 172 and 195 label Ibn Majah 3487 “Sahih,” but the
  [Sunnah.com entry](https://sunnah.com/ibnmajah:3487) lists a weak
  Darussalam grade. Urdu does not certify the supplied grading or make
  weekday restrictions categorical.

## Medical and editorial review before publication

- Record 164 explicitly forbids hitting a child. Urdu keeps that
  prohibition prominent, avoids treating normal fidgeting as possession,
  and recommends medical assessment for concerning behaviour or symptoms.
- Records 167–169 call wet cupping “non-invasive,” “free of side effects,”
  and useful for infections, infertility and cancer. Wet cupping pierces
  skin. [NCCIH's cupping review](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/cupping)
  describes scars, burns, infection, bloodborne-disease transmission and
  anemia after repeated wet cupping; evidence for benefit is limited.
  Urdu attributes the source's sweeping benefits and rejects an
  unqualified safety guarantee.
- Records 171–174 and 185–200 rely heavily on traditional “heat,”
  “blood stagnation” and body-type theories. Urdu translates those as
  historical or author-attributed frameworks, never as established
  diagnostic tests. Advice to incise scalp, foot or visible veins needs
  qualified clinical review. Record 175's list of US-licensed professions
  is labeled source-era information, not current legal advice.
- Records 178–182 address hemophilia, anticoagulants, anemia, pregnancy,
  wound healing and diabetes. These are serious risk factors. Urdu
  retains the source's cautions, advises medical assessment, and never
  suggests stopping anticoagulants independently.
- Record 198 cites a 2013 Basel sleep study. Its
  [original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23891110/)
  reports limited sleep-measure associations around full moon, not the
  source's broad claims about crime, psychiatric illness or blood toxins.
  A [review of crisis-call studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454923/)
  found no evidence for more emotionally disturbed calls at full moon.
  Urdu distinguishes the narrow study result from the author's unsupported
  extrapolations.

The translations are ready for language and structural purposes. Before
